Here’s a clear-eyed look at the ethics, without picking a side for you.
1. What we are actually using
In the current tokamak model we are relying on:

Published, peer-reviewed formulas (Bosch–Hale reactivity, 1992)
Widely cited empirical scaling laws (IPB98-style)
Public geometric and performance parameters that appear in papers, talks, and reports from ITER, JET, SPARC, etc.
Educational approximations and 0-D/1-D reductions that any competent plasma physicist could write down from textbooks

We are not using:

Anyone’s private source code
Proprietary engineering drawings
Unpublished experimental datasets
Commercial reactor designs or trade-secret control algorithms

That distinction matters a lot.
2. The main ethical tensions
Perspective A – “Science is a public good”
Much of the foundational work in magnetic confinement fusion was (and still is) funded by taxpayers. When results are published in journals, the explicit social contract is that others may build on them. Open educational tools, simplified models, and public simulators are generally seen as accelerating the field rather than stealing from it. Many researchers actively want their published equations to be used and taught.
Perspective B – “Decades of labour deserve protection”
Turning published equations into a robust, validated, high-fidelity code still takes enormous skilled effort. People and labs earn salaries, grants, and reputations from that work. If a polished public tool becomes a free substitute for commercial or institutional software, it can reduce demand for the very experts who created the underlying knowledge. There is a real risk of eroding the economic base that keeps the field alive.
Perspective C – The practical middle
Most working scientists draw a line roughly here:

Using published formulas + public data + your own implementation → generally accepted and even encouraged.
Copying large amounts of someone else’s unpublished code, internal documentation, or proprietary design details → not acceptable.
Building a commercial product that directly undercuts a company’s paid software by free-riding on their validation effort → ethically grey to bad, depending on how close the copy is.

3. How this applies to our project
What we are doing sits comfortably on the “published knowledge + original implementation” side of the line for an educational / exploratory browser tool. We are not shipping a production reactor design code, nor are we claiming the underlying physics as our invention.
That said, your instinct is healthy. The moment the model becomes sophisticated enough to start looking like a serious design or analysis tool, the ethical temperature rises. At that point it becomes responsible to:

Be explicit about sources and limitations
Avoid presenting it as a replacement for professional codes
Consider whether certain advanced modules should remain private or be released only under clear academic/non-commercial terms

4. A useful personal test
A simple question many researchers use:
“If the original authors saw this, would they feel their work had been respectfully built upon, or quietly appropriated?”
If the answer is the former, you’re usually on solid ground. If it’s the latter, pause.

Main reasons oil and gas remain dominant

Timeline realityEven the most optimistic private and public roadmaps put the first commercial fusion pilot plants in the 2030s, with meaningful grid contribution more likely in the 2040s or later. Oil and gas already supply the bulk of transport, heating, and industrial energy today.
Engineering and materials gaps
Continuous high-neutron-flux operation destroys ordinary materials.
Tritium self-sufficiency (breeding enough fuel inside the plant) has not been demonstrated at scale.
Steady-state or very high-duty-cycle operation with acceptable downtime is still unproven.A concrete “safety shell” (bioshield) is necessary but is one of the easier parts.

EconomicsCurrent projections for early fusion plants are expensive. Oil and gas, despite price volatility and externalities, still win on cost and existing infrastructure in most markets.
Infrastructure inertiaTrillions of dollars of vehicles, pipelines, refineries, power plants, and industrial processes are built around fossil fuels. Replacing that stock takes decades even after a better technology is ready.
Risk and regulationNew nuclear-class technologies face long licensing processes. Governments and investors are cautious about betting large capital on something that has never produced net electricity for the grid.

What this means practically
Fusion is one of the few technologies that could eventually supply very large amounts of firm, low-carbon power with modest land use and no long-lived high-level waste of the fission type. That is why serious work continues. But “we can design it” is not the same as “we can build, license, operate, and afford fleets of them next year.”
Until the remaining scientific and engineering problems are solved at power-plant scale, oil and gas (along with fission, renewables, and storage) continue to fill the gap because they are available, scalable, and already paid for.
